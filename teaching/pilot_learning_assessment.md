# Pilot learning assessment

Administer Form A before any teaching and Form B immediately after. Allocate about 8–10 minutes each. Learners use a randomly assigned pseudonymous code that links responses without names; do not publish individual responses or a code-to-name key. Optional delayed Form C is given 2–4 weeks later. Record time point, date, course and code. Keep any code linkage locally under course data procedures. Nonparticipation in evaluation must not prevent access to teaching.

## Form A before the session

The core assessment covers comparison planning. Before teaching, you can substitute the advanced versions of items 2 and 4 for a session covering estimation and simulation. The same version should be used at every time point, with the four-item, eight-point scale and the selected version reported.

1. A trial planned for 80% power gives p = .12. A colleague says this proves no treatment effect and that the sample-size calculation was wrong. Explain.
2. Core: a trial needs 56 analyzable patients per group and expects 5% loss. How many should be recruited per group, and why? Advanced alternative: a 95% interval for an improvement difference is 0.2 to 2.8 units. A 2-unit benefit matters clinically. What can be concluded?
3. A pilot has relief in 7/10 treated patients and 3/10 controls. Is 0.40 a sufficiently established effect to enter without sensitivity analysis? Explain.
4. Core: a clinically important benefit is 20 percentage points, but the trial is planned for 90% power at 30 points. Does it also have 90% power at 20 points? Explain. Advanced alternative: a simulation uses 50 patients per arm and 1,000 studies. What changes if studies increase to 10,000 while patients per arm remain 50?

## Form B immediately after the session

1. A trial planned for 90% power gives p = .18. Does that establish no effect or demonstrate an error in planning? Explain.
2. Core: a trial needs 80 analyzable patients per group and expects 20% loss. How many should be recruited per group, and why? Advanced alternative: a 95% interval for an improvement difference is 0.1 to 3.1 units. A 2-unit benefit matters clinically. What can be concluded?
3. A pilot observes improvement in 8/12 treated patients and 4/12 controls. How should its effect estimate inform planning?
4. Core: a 2-unit benefit matters clinically, but the trial is planned for 90% power at 3 units. Does that establish high power for every worthwhile benefit? Explain. Advanced alternative: a simulation uses 60 patients per arm and 500 studies. What changes if studies increase to 5,000 with the same patients per arm?

## Unfamiliar transfer case after teaching

Core: a rehabilitation study can analyze 40 patients per group. Its calculation requires 60 per group for 90% power at a 2-unit target difference, while a 1-unit benefit would matter clinically. Earlier effect estimates are uncertain. A short recommendation should explain the recruitment constraint, the difference at which power is evaluated, relevant alternative assumptions and a defensible next step.

Advanced estimation alternative: a study with 40 patients per group has an anticipated full interval width of 5 score units, while the intended decision needs width 2. A recommendation should explain the precision requirement, which assumptions determine width, and how the available information limits the conclusion.

## Form C delayed assessment

1. A study with target power .85 gives p = .14. Explain why this alone cannot establish no effect or inadequate planning.
2. Core: a trial needs 45 analyzable patients per group and expects 10% loss. How many should be recruited per group, and why? Advanced alternative: the improvement interval is 0.3 to 1.8 and the important benefit is 1 unit. How should it be interpreted?
3. A pilot of 15 per arm suggests a large effect. Identify one reason for caution and one sensitivity analysis.
4. Core: explain why a target difference should be justified as realistic and important, rather than selected just to obtain an affordable sample. Advanced alternative: distinguish increasing 100 participants per arm to 200 from increasing 1,000 simulation repetitions to 2,000.

## Scoring guide for educators

For each item: 0 incorrect/missing; 1 partly correct; 2 correct explanation including the key distinction. Total 8 at each time point. Item 1 concerns non-rejection and conditional power. Core item 2 requires division by the retained fraction and upward rounding: 59, 100 and 50 per group for Forms A, B and C. Advanced item 2 concerns an interval that excludes zero while allowing benefits below and above the clinical threshold. Item 3 concerns uncertain pilot estimates and the need for wider evidence and plausible alternatives. Advanced item 4 concerns increasing simulation repetitions while leaving each study's sample size and power unchanged.

For core item 4, credit the distinction between clinical importance and the difference at which power is evaluated: smaller worthwhile differences can have lower power. For Form C, credit importance and realism based on evidence, with feasibility assessed separately. Use the Monte Carlo key above only for the advanced version.

Score the transfer response 0–2 for each: goal; relevant assumptions; assessment at the available sample; justified recommendation with limitations (total 8). The core case concerns target difference, power and feasibility; the advanced case concerns confidence level, variability and interval width. Several recommendations may be defensible. Scorers should be blinded to time point where feasible and calibrate on two sample responses.

## Reporting

Report invited, attended, responding and paired counts; item-level and total-score distributions; paired descriptive changes; and missing-response patterns. Forms are locally developed and not psychometrically validated. Different item contexts, repeated exposure and an uncontrolled design limit interpretation. Treat confidence, enjoyment and usability as separate outcomes. A delayed assessment adds retention information but does not remove these limitations.
